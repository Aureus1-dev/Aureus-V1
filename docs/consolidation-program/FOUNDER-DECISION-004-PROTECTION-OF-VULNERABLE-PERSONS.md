# Founder Decision Brief 004 — Protection of Vulnerable Persons

**Status:** Decision brief only. No repository file edited. No constitutional language drafted. No documents merged.

---

## Current State

The preservation audit confirmed, by reading `OAS-006` (Rights, 20 Articles across 4 Parts), `OAS-007` (Duties, 23 Articles), `OAS-008` (Membership, 23 Articles), `OAS-009` (Governance, 18 Articles), `OAS-010` (Amendment, 18 Articles), and `OAS-011` (Justice, 18 Articles) in full, that **no constitutional document in the OAS lineage contains any protection specific to children, vulnerable persons, people experiencing hardship, or people with diminished capacity.** The closest adjacent text, `OAS-008` Art. 21 §1 (*"A community that strengthens its Members while discarding those who leave has not fulfilled the Collective's promise"*), addresses departing members specifically, under the Collective's promise, not vulnerability generally. This is a confirmed gap, not an unconfirmed suspicion.

## Evidence

`ALC-001` Art. IV §11 (Stewardship of the Vulnerable):

> *"Those who possess greater authority, knowledge, experience, or capability bear greater responsibility toward those who possess less. Children, vulnerable persons, and those experiencing hardship deserve particular care, patience, dignity, and protection. Institutional strength shall be measured not by how Aureus serves the strongest, but by how faithfully it serves those most in need."*

Relevant existing scaffolding this would attach to: `OAS-006` Art. 1 (Right to Dignity) is already the single strongest provision in the corpus — *"absolute. It admits no balancing against efficiency, mission progress, the flourishing of others, or any other right in this document."* `OAS-006`'s own stated method (Purpose section) is to *"translate that principle from an institutional duty into a person-held claim"* — precisely the operation a vulnerable-persons right would perform on the already-entrenched Dignity principle, applied to the population most exposed to its violation through neglect rather than intent. `OAS-006` also already creates rights *"established here for the first time"* using this identical reasoning (Privacy, Notice, the broadened anti-retaliation right) — this would not be a novel drafting move for this document, but a continuation of a pattern it already uses.

## Options

**Option A — Add as a constitutional right** (in `OAS-006`), following the same "established here for the first time" pattern already used for Privacy and Notice.

**Option B — Add as a constitutional duty** (in `OAS-007`) — a proactive obligation on Stewards/officers with greater capability, rather than a right the affected person must invoke.

**Option C — Add as both** — a right in `OAS-006` and a corresponding duty in `OAS-007`, mirroring how `OAS-006` Art. 15 and `OAS-007` Art. 12 already work as a matched pair for membership.

**Option D — Leave it to policy**, not the Constitution — addressed in operational/subordinate governance documents instead.

**Option E — Do not adopt at all.**

## Benefits

**Including it constitutionally (A/B/C):**
- Operationalizes the already-entrenched, absolute Dignity principle specifically for the population most likely to have it violated quietly, through neglect or oversight, rather than through a single identifiable bad act — exactly the failure mode a rights document organized around "the System shall never..." prohibitions is weakest at catching.
- Durability. A constitutional right or duty can only be narrowed through the CAP process (`OAS-010`), with Tribunal review and a recorded deliberation period. A policy-level protection can be revised or quietly weakened through ordinary operational change — a materially lower bar, applied to a protection that matters most exactly because the people it protects are least able to notice or contest its erosion themselves.
- Consistent with the institution's own stated measure of success (`ALC-001`'s framing, echoed in `OAS-004` Art. 4's Service principle) — an institution whose Mission is human flourishing arguably has this closer to its constitutional core than several rights already included.

**Option B specifically (duty, not just right):** Closes a structural weakness a rights-only approach would have. Every remedy mechanism in `OAS-011` (Notice, Explanation, Human Review, Petition) is **person-initiated** — the affected person must invoke it. The population this protection is meant to serve — children, people in hardship, people with diminished capacity — is disproportionately likely to be the population least able to self-petition. A purely reactive right, unaccompanied by a proactive institutional duty, may not actually protect this population in practice.

**Option D/E:** Avoids the risks below entirely; keeps constitutional text narrowly scoped to what's already settled.

## Risks

**Definitional risk (affects A/B/C):** Constitutional text is meant to be principle-level and durable; `OAS-005`'s entire definitional apparatus is built around avoiding operational specificity in constitutional text. Precisely defining "vulnerable," "hardship," or "diminished capacity" risks either (a) vagueness, undermining enforceability — `OAS-011` Art. 16 §2's own words: *"A constitutional order that declares rights and duties but supplies no means of adjudicating them has declared aspirations, not law"* — or (b) over-specificity, which risks under-inclusion (any enumerated list of categories will miss populations not anticipated at drafting time: e.g., the recently traumatized, non-native speakers navigating a complex system, the temporarily incapacitated).

**Tension with Human Agency (affects A/B/C, most acutely B):** `OAS-006` Art. 2 (Right to Agency) is itself entrenched, and `ALC-001`'s own text elsewhere warns: *"Guidance shall never become coercion. Assistance shall never become domination."* A poorly calibrated "protection of the vulnerable" provision risks drifting toward paternalism — treating a person's vulnerability as license to override their own decisions rather than as grounds for additional care offered, not imposed. This is a real risk to flag now, though resolving it is a drafting-stage concern, not a decision this brief needs to make.

**Institutional risk (Option B specifically):** A proactive duty is harder to bound than a reactive right — "shall exercise particular care" invites more interpretive dispute than "the person may petition for X." This is manageable (the corpus already handles duty-interpretation elsewhere, e.g. `OAS-007` Art. 21's rule that duties never condition rights) but is a real added complexity Option A alone would avoid.

**Option D/E risk:** Leaves the population least able to advocate for itself with the least durable protection available in the corpus — precisely backwards from where durability should be concentrated, if the goal is genuinely protecting those who can't easily protect themselves.

## Where such a principle would logically belong

**`OAS-006` (Rights), Part One or a new Part** — for the rights-holder's side, following the same drafting pattern already used for Privacy (Art. 5) and Notice (Art. 6): identified explicitly as newly established, not entrenched by default (consistent with `OAS-006` Art. 20 §2's own rule that only the Founding Charter can entrench text), unless the Founder specifically directs otherwise.

**`OAS-007` (Duties), Part One or Part Four** — for the corresponding proactive-care duty, if Option B or C is chosen, following the pattern `OAS-007` already uses for duties that mirror rights (Part One is explicitly organized this way).

Not `OAS-004` (Principles) — that document is explicitly reconciliatory/restating rather than newly enacting (its own Purpose section: *"creates no authority superior to the Founding Charter and entrenches nothing"*); a genuinely new protection is a better fit for `OAS-006`/`OAS-007`'s operative-rights-and-duties register than `OAS-004`'s principle-level one, consistent with how `OAS-006` itself already treats new rights.

## Long-Term Stewardship Considerations

This is precisely the category of protection `OAS-004` Art. 9 (Long-Term Stewardship) exists to safeguard against short-term neglect: *"no policy may be justified by benefits that arrive only by depleting what the System will need to serve those who come after."* An institution's treatment of its most vulnerable members is also the least visible in ordinary operating metrics and the easiest to let quietly erode under operational pressure (cost, scale, convenience) — exactly the failure mode constitutional-tier durability, rather than policy-tier flexibility, is suited to resist. Placed in `OAS-006`/`OAS-007` now, this protection would inherit the same amendment discipline (`OAS-010`) that already protects every other right in the corpus, meaning any future narrowing would require the same recorded, reviewed process — a meaningful long-term safeguard regardless of how the institution's leadership or scale changes.

## Recommendation

**Option C — both a right (in `OAS-006`) and a corresponding duty (in `OAS-007`).** The right alone would inherit the self-petition weakness every other `OAS-011`-enforced right has, which is a bigger problem for this population specifically than for most; the duty alone would lack the person-held claim and procedural protections (Notice, Explanation, Human Review, Petition) that make `OAS-006`'s other rights actually operative rather than aspirational. Together, they close the gap the way `OAS-006` Art. 15 / `OAS-007` Art. 12's membership pairing already demonstrates the corpus knows how to do. I'd flag two things explicitly for the drafting stage, not resolved here: keep the language deliberately general rather than an enumerated closed list, to avoid under-inclusion, and draft it in express, careful relationship to Human Agency (`OAS-006` Art. 2) so it reads as additional care offered, never as license to override a person's own decisions.
