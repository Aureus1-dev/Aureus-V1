# Founder Decision Brief 003 — Participation in Constitutional Governance

**Status:** Decision brief only. No repository file edited. No constitutional language drafted. No documents merged.

---

## Current State

`OAS-010` (Constitutional Amendment Process, Canonization, and Stewardship of the Constitutional Record — read in full for this brief and the prior verification pass) establishes the entire amendment process actually operative in the corpus today. It is **authority-decides, not participant-votes**:

1. Any person may *submit* a CAP (Constitutional Amendment Proposal) — Art. 3.
2. The CAP undergoes Tribunal review for entrenchment conflicts and a deliberation period *"during which the proposal is available for examination by any person"* — Art. 5 §1.
3. The **Canonizing Authority** — currently the Founder; after the founding period ends (a transition `OAS-009` Art. 16 establishes the mechanism for but does not itself trigger), the Council — alone decides to approve, reject, or return the proposal for revision — Art. 5 §1.

There is no step anywhere in `OAS-010` where a broader population of "Members" or "participants" casts a vote. "Available for examination by any person" is a transparency guarantee, not a voting right. This model is not an oversight — `OAS-009`'s entire separation-of-powers architecture (Council/Administration/Tribunal as three coequal, non-delegable bodies) is built around accountable institutional authority, and the ~200-file department Charter/Framework/SOP tree, plus `OAS-006` through `OAS-011` themselves, are all already constructed assuming this model.

## Evidence

`ALC-001` Art. VII ("Constitutional Decision-Making, Participation, and Amendment") assumes a materially different model: a defined body of *"eligible participants"* who vote on constitutional matters, with three response options:

> *"Approve. Do Not Approve. Not Yet Ready to Decide."* (§7)

> *"Choosing not to decide immediately shall never be treated as failure or disloyalty. Responsible stewardship sometimes requires additional learning."* (§7)

Also relevant: `ALC-001` Art. VII §3 attempts to bound this vote's scope — *"Only matters that materially affect the Constitution... shall require constitutional amendment procedures. Operational decisions shall not become constitutional matters merely because they are important"* — and §10 requires *"an exceptionally high standard of approval"* without specifying a number.

One fact changes since `ALC-001` and `OAS-001`'s lineage were drafted: **membership is now defined.** `OAS-008` (read in full for the preservation audit) resolved what was, at drafting time, an open gap flagged in nearly every earlier document's notes (`OAS-001` N2, `OAS-002` N2, `OAS-003` N2, `OAS-006` N1, `OAS-007` N1). A workable population for "eligible participant" — Members, as `OAS-008` Art. 1 defines them — now exists where it didn't when either document was written. This removes what would otherwise be the single largest practical obstacle to any participatory model.

## Options

**Option A — Keep the current model (authority-decides only), do not adopt the participatory concept.**

**Option B — Adopt full participatory voting**, replacing or supplementing the Canonizing Authority's decision with a binding Member vote, `ALC-001`-style, including the "Not Yet Ready to Decide" option.

**Option C — Advisory participation layer**: preserve `OAS-010`'s Canonizing Authority as the binding decision-maker, but formalize the existing "available for examination by any person" deliberation period into a structured input mechanism — Members may register a position, including an explicit "Not Yet Ready" signal — that informs but does not bind the Canonizing Authority's decision.

**Option D — Scoped participatory voting**: a binding Member vote, but only for a narrowly defined subset of amendments (e.g., non-entrenched, below some significance threshold), with everything else remaining authority-decides.

## Benefits

**Option A:** No re-engineering of an already-built, coherent system. Matches the accountability model `OAS-009`'s separation of powers was specifically designed around — a decision has one identifiable, answerable decision-maker. Fastest to operate. No new quorum, eligibility-verification, or threshold machinery needs inventing.

**Option B:** Broadest legitimacy and buy-in; most faithful to the "Collective" language already central to the mission (`OAS-001` Art. II §4 — member flourishing and institutional capacity as mutually reinforcing, not competing). Captures the humane "Not Yet Ready" design fully and directly.

**Option C:** Captures the Collective's participatory spirit and the "Not Yet Ready" concept's humane design without touching `OAS-009`/`OAS-010`'s already-built accountability architecture. Requires no immediate resolution of the threshold/quorum questions `OAS-010` itself flags as still open (Note N6: *"No canonical document currently specifies an actual supermajority threshold for any constitutional decision"*). Lowest implementation risk of the three participatory-leaning options.

**Option D:** A middle path — meaningful participation on lower-stakes matters, undiluted authority-based protection for entrenched/high-stakes ones.

## Risks

**Option A:** Forecloses the participatory concept `ALC-001` treats as load-bearing to institutional culture. As Aureus grows past the founding period, an authority-only model may face a legitimacy gap — decisions binding on a large community made entirely without that community's formal input.

**Option B:** The largest risk by far. `OAS-010`'s thresholds are genuinely unresolved (its own Note N6 flags this). A full participatory-vote layer risks: deadlock or capture by coordinated minorities; friction against `OAS-009`'s carefully built three-coequal-bodies separation of powers, which was not designed with a fourth, mass-participation power center in mind; and — most seriously — a functional collision with entrenchment. `OAS-001` Art. VII §3 makes certain provisions unamendable by ordinary process specifically to prevent them from being weakened by transient pressure; introducing a binding popular vote as the primary amendment mechanism, even if entrenched provisions remain formally protected, changes the political economy around them in ways the current architecture wasn't built to absorb.

**Option C:** Advisory input that's ignored repeatedly could itself become a legitimacy problem — "you asked but didn't listen" is worse, in some institutional-trust models, than never having asked. Requires discipline in practice, not just in text, to remain meaningful.

**Option D:** Requires defining, in the Constitution itself, what counts as a "lower-stakes" amendment eligible for a vote versus what doesn't — a line-drawing exercise that is itself a significant drafting task and a plausible source of future dispute (who decides which category a given CAP falls into?).

## Whether both ideas can coexist, and how

**Yes — Option C is the coexistence design.** It keeps `OAS-010`'s Canonizing Authority as the sole binding decision-maker (preserving `OAS-009`'s separation-of-powers architecture and the accountability line every downstream document already assumes), while formalizing the deliberation period `OAS-010` Art. 5 §1 already provides into a genuine participation mechanism: Members register a position — Approve, Do Not Approve, or explicitly Not Yet Ready — visible to the Canonizing Authority and the Tribunal before a decision is made. The "Not Yet Ready" option's core value (institutionally protecting the choice to defer judgment, explicitly not treating that as disloyalty) is fully preserved without requiring the Constitution to specify voting thresholds, quorum, or eligibility-verification machinery it doesn't currently have — the Canonizing Authority remains free to weigh strong "Not Yet Ready" sentiment as a signal to extend deliberation, without that being constitutionally mandatory.

Option D is a plausible second-best if the Founder wants binding participation specifically, but it requires solving a harder line-drawing problem than Option C does, and doesn't fit as cleanly with what `OAS-010` already built.

## Long-Term Stewardship Considerations

`OAS-009` Art. 16 already establishes the mechanism (not the act) for transferring the Founder's authority to the Council at the end of the founding period. That transition is a natural, already-anticipated point to revisit this question with more institutional experience behind it — Option C could be adopted now as the durable model, or explicitly framed as an interim arrangement pending a fuller participatory-governance document once the institution has operated long enough to know what actually works. Either framing is consistent with `OAS-004` Art. 9's Long-Term Stewardship principle (*"no policy may be justified by benefits that arrive only by depleting what the System will need to serve those who come after"*) — a lightweight, honest advisory mechanism that can mature into something more binding is lower-risk, long-term, than committing to full participatory machinery before the institution has the membership scale or operating history to make it work well.

## Recommendation

**Option C — the advisory participation layer.** It is the smallest change that captures everything genuinely valuable in `ALC-001`'s participatory concept (the Collective's participatory spirit, and specifically the humane, non-punitive "Not Yet Ready to Decide" design) without disturbing the accountable, already-built, already-adopted `OAS-009`/`OAS-010` architecture, and without requiring the Constitution to solve threshold/quorum problems it has explicitly and repeatedly deferred (`OAS-010` Note N6). It is also reversible in the low-risk direction — nothing about adopting Option C now forecloses moving to Option B or D later, once membership scale and operating experience make a fuller participatory model's tradeoffs clearer, ideally timed to the founding-period transition `OAS-009` already anticipates.
